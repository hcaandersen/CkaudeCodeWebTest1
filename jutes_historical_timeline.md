# Historical Timeline of the Jutes on the Jutland Peninsula (Until 700 AD)

## Bronze Age (c. 1500 - 500 BC)

**c. 1500 BC - Nordic Bronze Age Begins**
- The Nordic Bronze Age proper began around 1500 BC in Jutland and Scandinavia [1]
- A minimum of 50,000 burial mounds were constructed between 1500 and 1150 BC in Denmark alone, indicating significant population density [2]
- The period showed strong Mycenaean influences, with "foreign origins most consciously demonstrated in the formation of the Nordic Bronze Age Culture from 1500 BC onwards" [3]

**14th-13th Centuries BC**
- Oak coffin burials from this period contained well-preserved mummified bodies, along with their clothing and burial goods [4]
- Notable burial monuments include Borum Eshøj in eastern Jutland, one of the internationally most famous monuments from the Nordic Bronze Age [5]

## Pre-Roman Iron Age (500 BC - 1 AD)

**c. 500 BC - Transition to Iron Age**
- The end of the Bronze Age and start of the Pre-Roman Iron Age occurred around 500 BC [6]
- The period was characterized by cultural contact with the Central European La Tène culture (Celts) [7]
- During the Iron Age, Jutland was inhabited by the Jutes in North Jutland and the Angles in South Jutland, particularly in the region known as Angeln [8]

**c. 400-300 BC - Hjortspring Boat**
- The Hjortspring boat was built circa 400-300 BC, representing the oldest find of a wooden plank ship in Scandinavia [9]
- Discovered in Hjortspring Mose on the island of Als in southern Denmark, the vessel measured over 19 metres in length and could carry 24 warriors [10]
- The boat was deliberately deposited in a bog as a votive offering along with extensive weapons including 131 shields, 138 iron spearheads, and 11 single-edged iron swords [11]
- This represents the earliest evidence of maritime warriors and organized military forces in Denmark [12]

**c. 405-384 BC - Tollund Man**
- The Tollund Man died between 405-384 BC during the Pre-Roman Iron Age [13]
- His naturally mummified body was discovered in 1950 near Silkeborg on the Jutland peninsula [14]
- He died by hanging, possibly as a ritual sacrifice or punitive execution [15]

**120-115 BC - Cimbri and Teutones Migration Begins**
- Rising sea levels flooded the coastal lands of the Jutland Peninsula near the end of the 2nd century BCE [16]
- This environmental disaster induced the Cimbri (from northern Jutland/Himmerland) and Teutones to migrate southward seeking new lands [17]

**113-101 BC - Cimbrian War**
- The Cimbrian War was fought between the Roman Republic and the Germanic tribes of the Cimbri and Teutons who migrated from the Jutland peninsula [18]
- 105 BC: Battle of Arausio - One of the greatest disasters in Roman military history, with the Cimbri and Teutones defeating Roman forces [19]
- 102 BC: Battle of Aquae Sextiae - Roman general Gaius Marius defeated the Teutones [20]
- 101 BC: Battle of Vercellae - The Cimbri faced annihilation at the hands of Marius and Quintus Lutatius Catulus [21]

## Roman Iron Age (1-400 AD)

**98 AD - First Written Reference to the Jutes**
- The Roman historian Publius Cornelius Tacitus mentions the "Eudoses" (Eudosians) in his work Germania, chapter 40 [22]
- The Eudoses were listed among Germanic tribes including the Anglians, protected by forests and rivers [23]
- Modern scholars believe "Jutes" is an Anglified version of the ancient name "Eudoses" [24]
- The Old English word "Eote" for the Eudoses is etymologically related to "eoten," meaning giants [25]

**1-400 AD - Roman Iron Age Period**
- The Roman Iron Age describes the period of major Roman Empire influence on Scandinavia [26]
- Denmark was densely populated during the older Roman Iron Age, with villages close together (1-2 km apart) [27]
- The land was divided between warlike and intensely rivaling tribes who built extensive fortifications [28]
- Extensive Roman goods were imported, including more than 7,000 coins, vessels, bronze images, glass beakers, enameled buckles, and weapons [29]

**Late 3rd Century BC (found from this period)**
- Grauballe Man, dating from the late 3rd century BC during the early Germanic Iron Age, was killed by having his throat slit and deposited in a bog [30]
- Evidence suggests human sacrifice practices continued during the Roman Iron Age [31]

**c. 200 AD - Danish Expansion**
- By 200 AD, the Danish people had begun using the Rune language chiseled in stone [32]
- The Danes had established trade links with the Roman Empire, trading goods such as animal furs and amber [33]
- Danish expansion into Jutland began, with some Jutes being absorbed by Danish culture while others may have migrated to northern Francia and Frisia [34]

**250-475 AD - Nydam Sacrificial Deposits**
- Archaeological analysis indicates sacrificial deposits of weapons were made at Nydam Mose in several distinct phases between AD 250 and AD 475 [35]
- Following local battles, defeated enemies' weapons and boats were sunk in a lake as sacrifices [36]

**310-320 AD - Nydam Boat**
- The famous Nydam oak boat was constructed and has been dendrochronologically dated to 310-320 CE [37]
- The boat measured 23 meters long, 3.5 meters wide, had 14 pairs of oars, and likely had a crew of around 45 men [38]
- Conrad Engelhardt discovered the Nydam Boat on August 17, 1863 [39]

**3rd-5th Centuries AD - Migration to Frisian Coast (Hypothesis)**
- One hypothesis suggests that after Danish invasion of Jutland, some Jutes migrated to the Frisian coast [40]
- From the 3rd through 5th centuries, Frisia suffered marine transgressions that made most of the land uninhabitable [41]
- The Jutes were among those who settled temporarily on the Frisian coast before their later migration to Britain [42]

## Migration Period (400-700 AD)

**c. 430 AD - First Germanic Mercenaries in Britain**
- Archaeological evidence and the Gallic Chronicle of 452 indicate Germanic mercenaries arrived in Britain around 430 AD [43]
- The Romano-British leader Vortigern invited Germanic mercenaries (including Saxons and Jutes) to help defend against Picts and Scots [44]
- Vortigern initially granted them the Isle of Thanet, and eventually Kent was ceded to Jutish leaders [45]

**449 AD - Traditional Date of Hengist and Horsa's Arrival**
- According to Bede's Ecclesiastical History and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Hengist and Horsa arrived in Kent around 449 AD with three keels of warriors [46]
- They were identified as sons of Wictgils, descending from Woden, and leaders of Jutish forces [47]
- The brothers landed at Eopwinesfleot (Ebbsfleet) in Kent [48]
- Modern scholarship regards Hengist and Horsa as likely mythical figures, though the migration they represent was historical [49]

**5th Century - Jutish Settlement in Britain**
- Large-scale Jutish migration from Denmark (and possibly the Frisian coast) to Britain occurred during the later 5th century [50]
- According to the Venerable Bede, the Jutes settled in Kent, the Isle of Wight, and parts of Hampshire (the Meonwara region) [51]
- The peoples known as the Cantware (Kent), Wihtware (Isle of Wight), and Meonwara (Hampshire) were established [52]

**c. 450-500 AD - Depopulation of Jutland**
- Large parts of the Jutish population migrated towards Britannia during the 5th century [53]
- Those who remained in Jutland were gradually absorbed by the Danes [54]

**c. 534-580 AD - Eormenric of Kent**
- Eormenric, descended from Hengist, reigned as King of Kent from approximately 534/540 to 564/580 [55]
- He can be regarded as the first historical King of Kent, as Gregory of Tours recorded his son Æthelberht's marriage while filius regis (son of the king) [56]
- His name "Eormen-" suggests connections to the Frankish kingdom, as this element was rare among Anglo-Saxons but common among Frankish nobles [57]

**6th Century - Procopius's Account**
- The Byzantine historian Procopius (writing mid-6th century) described an island called "Brittia" inhabited by Angles, Frisians, and Britons [58]
- Notably, Procopius did not mention Saxons or Jutes by name, indicating the complexity of tribal identifications during this period [59]

**c. 589-616 AD - Æthelberht of Kent**
- Æthelberht, son of Eormenric, became King of Kent around 589 and ruled until his death on February 24, 616 [60]
- He married Bertha, the Christian daughter of Charibert I, king of the Franks, building an alliance with the most powerful state in Western Europe [61]
- Æthelberht was the first Anglo-Saxon king to convert to Christianity [62]

**597 AD - Gregorian Mission to Kent**
- Pope Gregory the Great sent Augustine of Canterbury with approximately 40 missionaries to England in 596, arriving in 597 [63]
- Augustine arrived at Thanet and was received by King Æthelberht, who granted him permission to preach and a dwelling-place in Canterbury [64]
- Kent, ruled by the Jutes, became the entry point for Christianity into Anglo-Saxon England [65]
- Gregory reportedly stated that over ten thousand people were baptized at Christmas 597 [66]

**601 AD - Pope Gregory's Letters**
- Pope Gregory sent multiple letters to Augustine, including instructions on converting pagan temples to Christian use (Register 11.56) [67]
- Gregory referred to all Germanic peoples in Britain collectively as "Angli," not distinguishing between Jutes, Angles, and Saxons [68]

**602-603 AD - Æthelberht's Law Code**
- Æthelberht issued the earliest written code in any Germanic language for Kent [69]
- The laws instituted a complex system of fines and were among the first documents written down in Anglo-Saxon [70]

**7th Century - Absorption of Jutish Identity**
- By the 7th century, the Jutes gradually lost their distinct identity and were absorbed into broader Anglo-Saxon society [71]
- The Jutish territories in Kent, the Isle of Wight, and Hampshire became integral parts of larger political entities [72]
- The distinctions between Jutes, Angles, and Saxons became less pronounced as these groups intermarried and developed a unified "English" identity [73]

**Late 7th Century - Wessex Dominance**
- The Jutish regions, including Kent, Meonwara, and Ytene, gradually came under the dominance of larger Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, particularly Wessex [74]
- By this time, the people of Jutland who had remained in Denmark had been fully absorbed into Danish culture [75]

## Notes on Sources and Methodology

This timeline synthesizes information from multiple web sources accessed in October 2025, including Wikipedia articles, archaeological websites, university research portals, and historical databases. All dates marked with "c." (circa) are approximate, reflecting the uncertainties inherent in ancient and early medieval chronology. The Pre-Roman Iron Age and Roman Iron Age periodization follows standard Scandinavian archaeological conventions.

The connection between Tacitus's "Eudoses" and the later "Jutes" remains a scholarly hypothesis rather than established fact. Similarly, the historicity of figures like Hengist and Horsa is debated, though they likely represent genuine historical migrations even if the specific individuals are legendary.
