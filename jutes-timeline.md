# Timeline of Historical Events Related to the Jutes (Until 700 AD)

## Early 400s CE - Coastal Flooding and Migration Pressure

**c. 400-430 CE: Mass Flooding in North Germany and Frisia**
- Archaeological evidence and scientific research indicates that the early 400s saw mass flooding on the coasts of North Germany and Frisia, forcing the Jutes to migrate en-masse across the narrow expanse of the sea into Britain [1][2].
- During the late 300s and early 400s AD, boat-loads of Germanic people (including Jutes, Angles, Saxons, and Frisians) started crossing the North Sea to the eastern shores of Britain [3].
- Settlements were abandoned between AD 400-600, with one explanation being the deteriorating climate after the Roman Optimum (AD 250-400) [4].
- Previously inhabited sites on the Frisian and north German coasts had been rendered uninhabitable by flooding, forcing the migration due to displacement [5].

## Mid-5th Century - Arrival and Settlement in Britain

**449 CE: Invitation by Vortigern**
- According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Hengist and Horsa, legendary leaders of the Jutes, were invited to Sub-Roman Britain by Vortigern to help his forces fight the Picts [6][7].
- They landed at Wippidsfleet (Ebbsfleet) on the Isle of Thanet in east Kent [8][9].
- They defeated the Picts wherever they fought them [10].
- Bede records that Hengist and Horsa were the first chieftains among the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes in England [11].

**455 CE: Battle of Aylesford**
- Hengist and Horsa fought against Vortigern at Aylesford [12][13].
- Horsa was killed in this battle [14].
- Following Horsa's death, Hengist took control of the kingdom along with his son Esc (also spelled Æsc or Oisc) [15].

**c. 455-500 CE: Establishment of Jutish Territories**
- The Jutes settled in Kent, the Isle of Wight, and parts of Hampshire [16][17].
- According to Bede: "From the Jutes are descended the people of Kent, and of the Isle of Wight, and those also in the province of the West Saxons who are to this day called Jutes, seated opposite to the Isle of Wight" [18].
- In Hampshire, Jutes founded kingdoms known as Wihtwara (Isle of Wight), Meonwara (Meon Valley), and Ytene (in an area similar to the later site of the New Forest) [19][20].
- The Kingdom of Kent was founded around the middle of the 5th century, when Roman ways and influences must have still had a strong presence [21].

**488 CE: Esc Becomes King of Kent**
- According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Esc (Æsc/Oisc) succeeded to the kingdom and was king of the men of Kent for twenty-four winters [22].
- After Esc, the kings of Kent were known as Oiscings [23].

## 6th Century - Archaeological Evidence and Kingdom Development

**Early-Mid 5th to Late 6th Century: Archaeological Evidence**
- Brooches and bracteates found in east Kent, the Isle of Wight, and southern Hampshire showed strong Frankish and North Sea influence from the mid-fifth century to the late sixth century, compared to north German styles found elsewhere in Anglo-Saxon England [24].
- There is archaeological evidence of Kentish trade links in Hampshire and the Isle of Wight [25].
- A large early Jutish cemetery dating from early 5th to mid 6th century was discovered at Deal, Kent, containing around 40 individuals [26].
- One burial at Deal contained a male individual buried beside a horse and dog grave, with a shield boss, spear, ring sword with gold and glass garnet, annular brooch, knife, and bronze bucket [27].
- Another Jutish warrior was buried around 550 AD with his sword and shield at Deal [28].
- Droxford in the Meon valley was the site of a large Jutish cemetery [29].
- On the Isle of Wight, the two main Jutish cemetery sites were at Chessell Down and Bowcombe Down [30].

**c. 512-540 CE: Octa's Reign**
- Octa succeeded Esc as King of Kent and reigned until his death in 540 or 543 CE [31].

**c. 540-c. 590 CE: Eormenric's Reign**
- Eormenric (also spelled Irminric) became King of Kent following his father Octa's death in 543 [32].
- Gregory of Tours infers he was still alive in 589 [33].
- Eormenric was the father of Æthelberht [34].

**c. 580 CE: Marriage of Æthelberht and Bertha**
- Æthelberht of Kent married Bertha, the Christian daughter of Charibert I, king of the Franks [35].
- The marriage was on condition that she be allowed to practice her Christian religion [36].
- She brought her chaplain, Liudhard, with her to England [37].
- A former Roman church was restored for Bertha just outside Canterbury and dedicated to Martin of Tours [38].
- This marriage built an alliance with the most powerful state in contemporary Western Europe [39].

**c. 589-616 CE: Æthelberht's Reign**
- Æthelberht became King of Kent about 589 and reigned until his death on February 24, 616 [40].
- As bretwalda, he wielded significant influence over other Anglo-Saxon kings in the late sixth century [41].
- Bede gives his genealogy: "Ethelbert was son of Irminric, son of Octa, and after his grandfather Oeric, surnamed Oisc, the kings of the Kentish folk are commonly known as Oiscings. The father of Oeric was Hengist" [42].

## Late 6th - Early 7th Century - Christianization

**595 CE: Pope Gregory Plans the Mission**
- Pope Gregory the Great chose Augustine, prior of Gregory's own monastery of St Andrew in Rome, to head a mission to Britain to Christianize King Æthelberht and his Kingdom of Kent [43].
- Gregory wrote to one of the papal estate managers in southern Gaul, asking that he buy English slave boys so that they might be educated in monasteries, which historians interpret as early planning for the mission [44].

**596 CE: The Missionaries Depart Rome**
- Augustine and approximately 40 companions departed Rome, but soon halted, daunted by the task [45].
- Augustine returned to Rome to request papal permission to abandon the mission, but Gregory refused and sent Augustine back with letters encouraging the missionaries to persevere [46].

**597 CE: Arrival of the Gregorian Mission**
- Augustine and his companions landed on the Isle of Thanet in east Kent [47].
- According to Bede, Æthelberht was sufficiently distrustful of the newcomers to insist on meeting them under the open sky, to prevent them from performing sorcery [48].
- The monks impressed Æthelberht, but he was not converted immediately [49].
- He agreed to allow the mission to settle in Canterbury and permitted them to preach [50].
- Neither Bede nor Gregory mentions the exact date of Æthelberht's conversion, but it probably took place in 597 [51].
- Augustine was consecrated as a bishop and converted many of the king's subjects, including thousands during a mass baptism on Christmas Day in 597 [52].
- Augustine became the first archbishop of Canterbury in 597 [53].
- Soon after his arrival, Augustine founded the monastery of Saints Peter and Paul (later St Augustine's Abbey) on land donated by King Æthelberht [54].

**598 CE: Mass Conversions**
- In a letter Gregory wrote to the patriarch of Alexandria in 598, he claimed that more than 10,000 Christians had been baptized [55].

**601 CE: Pope Gregory's Letter to Queen Bertha**
- Pope Gregory addressed a letter to Bertha, in which he complimented her highly on her faith and knowledge of letters [56].

## 7th Century - Political Developments and Conflicts

**616 CE: Death of Æthelberht, Succession of Eadbald**
- Æthelberht died on February 24, 616 [57].
- His son Eadbald succeeded him as King of Kent (616-640) [58].
- Eadbald was the son of King Æthelberht and his wife Bertha [59].
- Eadbald's accession was a significant setback for the growth of the church, since he retained his people's paganism and did not convert to Christianity for at least a year, and perhaps for as many as eight years [60].
- Eadbald had two sons, Eormenred and Eorcenberht, and a daughter, Eanswith [61].

**640 CE: Eorcenberht Becomes King**
- Eorcenberht (also Ærconberht, Earconberht, or Earconbert) succeeded his father Eadbald as King of Kent [62].
- According to Bede, Eorcenberht was the first king in Britain to command that pagan "idols" (cult images) be destroyed and that Lent be observed, marking a key point in the Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England [63].

**664 CE: Ecgberht I Becomes King**
- Eorcenberht died on July 14, 664 [64].
- His son Ecgberht I (also spelled Egbert) succeeded him as King of Kent (664-673) [65].

**673 CE: Death of Ecgberht I**
- Ecgberht I died on July 4, 673 [66].

**c. 680s: Expansion of Wessex into Jutish Territories**
- Caedwalla, probably concerned at Mercian and South Saxon influence in Southern England, conquered the land of the South Saxons and took over the Jutish areas in Hampshire and the Isle of Wight in the late 680s [67].

**686 CE: Conquest of the Isle of Wight**
- Caedwalla became king of the West Saxons in 685 or 686 after his predecessor Centwine retired to a monastery [68].
- In 686, Caedwalla conquered the Isle of Wight, whose pagan inhabitants he annihilated [69][70].
- The Jutes of the Isle of Wight were still pagan; in fact, this island was the last bit of England to receive the Christian faith [71].
- He slaughtered the inhabitants, who were heathen Jutes, and settled West Saxons there instead [72].
- Caedwalla killed Aruald (also spelled Arwald), the king of the Isle of Wight [73].
- Aruald's two younger brothers, who were heirs to the throne, escaped from the island but were hunted down and found at Stoneham, Hampshire, where they were killed on Cædwalla's orders [74].
- Once the campaign had begun, he was persuaded to make over a quarter of the island (three hundred hides of land) to the Church [75].
- In this conquest he was associated with the great bishop Wilfrid [76].
- The Jutish territories around the present city of Southampton were seized by Cædwalla, king of Wessex, c. 686 [77].

**688 CE: Caedwalla's Abdication**
- Caedwalla was badly wounded during his war on the Isle of Wight [78].
- He abdicated in 688 and traveled to Rome, where he was baptized [79].

**689 CE: Death of Caedwalla**
- Caedwalla died in Rome in 689, shortly after his baptism [80].

## Context and Historical Sources

**731 CE: Bede Completes His Historia Ecclesiastica**
- Bede completed his *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* (*Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum*) in 731 [81].
- It is the first account of Anglo-Saxon England ever written and remains the single most valuable source for this period [82].
- According to Bede's account, the withdrawal of Roman rule in the fifth century ushered in a period of great instability with incursions by the Scots and Picts, then the Germanic peoples: Angles, Saxons, and Jutes [83].
- Bede said the Saxons, Jutes, and Angles came from different parts of Germany and Denmark – the Jutes were from Jutland, now part of Denmark [84].
- The work details the history of the conversion of the English to Christianity from the time of St Augustine through to the early eighth century [85].

## Historical Debates and Notes

**Origins of the Jutes:**
- There is no consensus amongst historians on the origins of the Jutes [86].
- One hypothesis is that they originated from the Jutland Peninsula, but after a Danish invasion of that area, migrated to the Frisian coast [87].
- The Jutes have no recorded history on the European continent, but there is considerable evidence that their home was in the Scandinavian area (probably Jutland) and that those who did not migrate were later absorbed by the Danes [88].
- They were possibly or probably related to the North Germanic tribe Geats [89].

**Reliability of Early Dates:**
- Bede supplies the date 449, which was traditionally accepted but has been considered suspect since the late 20th century [90].
- Modern historians consider Hengist and Horsa "semi-legendary" figures, with their story likely containing both historical elements and mythological additions [91].
- The earlier dates provided by the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle have been contested by some findings in archaeology [92].
- The first of the Kent kings' names that can be placed historically with reasonable confidence is Æthelberht's father, Eormenric [93].

**Political Status in Later Centuries:**
- The kingdom of Kent, prominent in the early 7th century, found it hard to cope with powerful neighbours and during the 8th century was taken over by Mercia and in the 9th by Wessex [94].
- According to St Bede, the Jutes were conquered by the surrounding Saxon kingdoms during the 7th century [95].
- By the 7th century, their territories in Kent and the Isle of Wight had become integral parts of larger political entities [96].
