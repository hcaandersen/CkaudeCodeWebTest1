# Post‑Germanic Common Sense

*By Hans Andersen*

Westerners often speak as if their “common sense” were universal—transparent to reason, obvious to any clear-eyed person. But common sense is not a law of nature; it is a cultural inheritance. This essay introduces **post‑Germanic** as a term for one of the key lineages shaping Western habits of thought: the aristocratic, warrior‑landholding logic that emerged from the Germanic migrations of the 5th–8th centuries and was then absorbed into medieval and modern institutions. The claim is not ethnic but conceptual: a continuity of **mindset**—hierarchy, loyalty, property, conquest—as a taken‑for‑granted moral order.

The argument proceeds in three moves. First, I sketch how Germanic warrior culture crystallised into aristocratic structures in medieval Europe. Second, I trace how this logic migrated from feudalism into modern corporatism—not as a one‑to‑one mapping of titles, but as a **structure of thought**. Third, I show why this matters when Western leaders interpret other civilisations: they often assume that others will use power as they themselves would, and they misread the world as a result.

---

## 1) From War‑Band to Aristocracy

The early Germanic *comitatus*—the retinue bound to a leader by oaths, gifts and honour—made loyalty a personal virtue and war a social glue (Tacitus, *Germania*). Over time, as Germanic peoples (Angles, Saxons, Franks, Goths, Lombards) took Roman provinces, their war‑band ethos fused with Christian kingship and Roman legal forms. The result was a hybrid **aristocratic order**: land for service, oaths for protection, honour for status—an economy of power that rewarded fidelity and prowess (Wallace‑Hadrill; Bloch).

Feudalism was not a simple Roman survival; it carried strong traces of that warrior substrate: decentralised power, patrimonial landholding, and personal bonds of lord and vassal. What mattered for our purposes is the **moral psychology** this system normalised: hierarchy as natural, property as sacralised, violence as legitimate when ordered through rank and oath.

---

## 2) Post‑Germanic as a Mindset

By **post‑Germanic** I mean this: a continuing **style of reasoning about order** inherited from the Germanic‑feudal synthesis, later reframed in religious, legal and then secular terms. Its everyday axioms include:

* Property is personal and inviolable.
* Order arises from ranked authority and loyal service.
* Ambition is moralised as progress; subordination as duty.
* **Conquest proves success**—victory (military, commercial, technological) is treated as evidence of merit.

Much Western “common sense” is simply this moral architecture made invisible by familiarity.

---

## 3) From Feudalism to Corporatism: The Persistence of the Post‑Germanic Mind

The end of feudal tenure did not end its mental world. What endured was not titles but a **way of thinking about hierarchy**—that power, properly exercised, is both rational and moral. Modern corporatism preserves this logic in abstract form. Authority is justified by perceived competence; control presents itself as stewardship; competition is imagined to reveal virtue; and success is taken as proof of rightness.

Historically, the bridge was built by the **chartered companies** of the early modern period (VOC, East India Company). They married aristocratic privilege (exclusive charters, monopolies, martial force) to mercantile rationality (ledgers, boards, audit), exporting a corporate‑colonial model of rule. The corporation thus became a post‑feudal machine for hierarchy and extraction, secularising the old ethic of conquest into market expansion and managerial order.

The point is philosophical, not schematic. We need not say “lord = CEO.” Rather: the **moral grammar** that once bound lord and vassal—ranked loyalty, duty upward for protection downward, success as validation—still animates Western institutions and self‑conceptions. It is this grammar I call post‑Germanic.

---

## 4) Why This Matters Beyond the West

Western leaders often presume that other civilisations will wield power as they themselves would: rationally, institutionally, and with the stewardship logic of ranked responsibility. That presumption is itself post‑Germanic. Many societies organise legitimacy differently—through kinship networks, ritual authority, sacral kingship, bureaucratic paternalism, or civilisational narratives in which power is symbolic before it is instrumental. When Western actors project their own moral grammar outward, they mistake difference for dysfunction and strategy for incoherence.

Recognising the post‑Germanic cast of Western common sense is therefore a discipline of self‑knowledge. It does not deny the Roman inheritance (law, citizenship, administration) or the Christian one (universalism, conscience), but it insists that the **warrior‑aristocratic** thread is equally formative—and that it still colours how the West reasons about property, order, ambition and legitimacy.

---

## 5) Caveats and Directions for Inquiry

* **Plural inheritance.** Western institutions are a braid of Roman, Christian, Germanic and later Enlightenment strands; no single thread explains all outcomes.
* **Regional variance.** The post‑Germanic grammar took different shapes in England, France, the German lands and Iberia; its colonial afterlives differ again.
* **Analytic use, not essence.** *Post‑Germanic* names a heuristic for mindset continuity, not an ethnic essence or a closed historical period.
* **Comparative work.** To understand misreadings (e.g., of China), one must place Western post‑Germanic assumptions alongside Confucian statecraft, Legalist traditions, imperial tributary logics, and modern party‑state ideologies.

---

## Conclusion

**Post‑Germanic** names a habit of mind inherited from Europe’s warrior‑aristocratic past: hierarchy moralised as order, property sacralised as self, ambition redeemed as progress, and victory read as virtue. Feudalism gave this ethic its medieval form; corporatism abstracts it for the modern age. To see it is to loosen its spell. And once we see it, we can meet other civilisations without presuming that our way of using power is the obvious—or the only—way.
