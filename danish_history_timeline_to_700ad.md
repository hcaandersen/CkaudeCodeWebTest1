# Timeline of Danish History: From Prehistory to 700 AD

## Paleolithic Period (c. 10,000 BC - 9,000 BC)

**c. 10,000 BC**: End of the Last Ice Age
- First human settlement in Denmark begins as hunters and fishermen enter the region, migrating from Southern and Eastern Europe [1][2]
- The land that would become Denmark emerges from ice age conditions, becoming suitable for human habitation

## Mesolithic Period (c. 9,000 BC - 4,000 BC)

**c. 9,600-6,500 BC**: Maglemose Culture
- The Maglemosian culture (c. 9000 – c. 6000 BC) represents the earliest Mesolithic culture of Denmark and South Scandinavia [3][4]
- Named after the archaeological site Maglemose near Gørlev and Høng on western Zealand
- People lived in forest and wetland environments, using fishing and hunting tools made from wood, bone, and flint microliths [3]
- First evidence of domesticated dogs in Denmark [3]
- Characteristic sharply edged microliths of flintstone used for spear and arrow heads [3]
- First settlement of the culture excavated in 1900 by George Sarauw [3]
- Many settlements now lie underwater due to sea level changes; during this period Britain was connected to mainland Europe

**c. 5,400-3,950 BC**: Ertebølle Culture
- Hunter-gatherer and fisher, pottery-making culture dating to the end of the Mesolithic period [5][6]
- Named after the type site at Ertebølle village on Limfjorden in Jutland
- Major excavations in 1893-97 and 1980-84 revealed one of Europe's largest shell heaps: 140 metres long, 20 metres wide, up to 1.9 metres deep, primarily consisting of oyster shells [5]
- Early phase: c. 5,400 BCE – c. 4,500 BCE
- Later phase: c. 4,500 BCE – 3,950 BCE [5]
- Shortly after 4,100 BCE the Ertebølle began to expand along the Baltic coast as far as Rügen [5]
- The Ertebølle culture replaced the earlier Kongemose culture of Denmark [5]
- During the 5th millennium BCE, the Ertebølle people learned pottery from neighbouring tribes in the south [7]
- Many coastal sites are now submerged 3-4 meters underwater, excavated by underwater archaeology [5]
- Important underwater sites include Tybrind Vig with well-preserved canoes and paddles [8]
- Denmark has the richest concentration of underwater Stone Age finds in the world [8]

## Neolithic Period (c. 4,000 BC - 1,700 BC)

**c. 4,000-2,800 BC**: Funnel Beaker Culture (TRB)
- Around 4000 BCE, the Ertebølle people became part of the megalithic Funnelbeaker culture [7]
- Named after the distinct funnel-shaped beakers they produced, this Neolithic archaeological culture existed between around 4000 and 2800 BCE [7]
- Farms began to appear on flat, fertile land; farmers initially used stone tools and weapons [1]
- By 3000 BC, agricultural settlements were well established [1]
- Most extensive construction of megalithic monuments in Denmark occurred in the second half of the 4th millennium BC [9]
- Thousands of unchambered long barrows, dolmens, passage graves and causewayed enclosures were built [9]

**c. 3,600-3,400 BC**: Early Megalithic Construction
- Practice of using large stones (megaliths) for burial chambers was present by 3600 cal BC [9]
- Classical Urdolmen were built alongside various types of more complex dolmen chambers during ca. 3600-3400 cal BC [9]
- These structures required communal effort and organization, suggesting social hierarchy [9]

**c. 3,400 BC onward**: Passage Graves Era
- After 3400 cal BC, passage graves were erected [9]
- Dolmen types were followed by passage graves, which were concealed within earthen mounds [9]
- Megalithic tombs indicate not only spiritual significance but also structured community organization [9]

**c. 3,000 BC**: Major Neolithic Archaeological Discovery
- A 5,000-year-old Neolithic site (from our perspective in 2025) has been unearthed at Nygårdsvej 3, near Eskilstrup on the island of Falster [7]
- Site revealed unusual stone-paved cellar associated with the Beaker Culture
- Stone paved sunken floors were previously unknown from Neolithic Denmark [7]
- Excavation revealed two phases of house construction using designs common to the Funnel Beaker Culture [7]
- Discovery of a timber circle in Denmark that may shed light on Bell Beaker-era ritual practices and connections to sites like Stonehenge [7]

**c. 2,850-2,250 BC**: Single Grave Culture / Battle Axe Culture
- The Single Grave culture was a local variant of the Corded Ware culture, emerging from migration of peoples from the Pontic-Caspian steppe [10][11]
- Traditional dating suggests it succeeded the Funnel Beaker culture around 2800 BC [11]
- Characterized by single burial practices [11]
- Deceased usually accompanied by battle axe, amber beads, and pottery vessels [11]
- Chronology:
  - Younger Neolithic I: Lower Grave Period (2850-2600 BC)
  - Younger Neolithic II: Lower Grave Period (2600-2450 BC)
  - Younger Neolithic III: Upper Grave Period (2450-2250 BC) [11]
- Males typically buried with battle axes, large amber discs and flint tools [11]
- Females buried with amber necklaces made of small beads [11]
- Both genders buried with ceramic beakers [11]

**c. 2,000 BC**: Population Turnover
- Major genetic discontinuity: Neolithic Stone Age people who brought farming to Denmark lived there for only 1,000 years before being replaced by Yamnaya-related immigrants with ancestry from the Pontic Steppe [8]
- Denmark's population has totally turned over twice during prehistoric times [8]

## Bronze Age (c. 1,700 BC - 500 BC)

**c. 2,000/1,750-500 BC**: Nordic Bronze Age
- The Nordic Bronze Age culture emerged in the period 2000-1750 BC as a continuation of the Late Neolithic Dagger period, with influences from Central Europe [1][12]
- The Bronze Age in Denmark covers the period 1700-500 BC [1]
- Farms had begun to appear, and farmers adopted bronze for tools and weapons [1]

**c. 1,700-1,300 BC**: Evidence of Horse-Drawn Chariots
- Evidence for horse-drawn chariots first appears in Scandinavia around 1700 BC, in the form of petroglyphs, chariot bits and whip handles [13]
- Thousands of rock art images on about 600 panels at Tanum, Sweden (near Denmark) [13]

**c. 1,400 BC**: Trundholm Sun Chariot
- The Trundholm sun chariot, a Nordic Bronze Age artifact, dated to about 1400 BC or Montelius period II (c. 1500-1300 BC) [13]
- Discovered in 1902 in a peat bog on the Trundholm moor in northwestern Zealand
- Bronze statue of a horse and large bronze disk (about 25cm diameter) placed on a device with spoked wheels [13]
- Approximately 54 cm × 35 cm × 29 cm in size
- The sun disc is gilded on one side [13]
- Cast using the lost wax method [13]
- Perhaps the finest example of Scandinavian Bronze Age religion: representing the cosmic passage of the sun through heavenly light and darkness of the underworld [13]
- Now in the collection of the National Museum of Denmark in Copenhagen [13]

**c. 850-760 BC**: Minor Climate Change
- A minor change in climate occurred between 850 BC and 760 BC, introducing a wetter, colder climate [14]
- This affected the late Bronze Age period

**c. 650 BC**: Significant Climate Deterioration
- A more radical climate change began around 650 BC [14]
- These climatic shifts contributed to the end of the Nordic Bronze Age period

## Pre-Roman Iron Age (c. 500 BC - 1 AD)

**c. 500 BC**: Transition to Iron Age
- Around 500 BC people began to extract iron from local deposits [1][15]
- People were no longer dependent on bronze from distant areas of Europe [1]
- Iron was much stronger and more suitable metal for weapons and tools [1]
- The climate in Denmark and southern Scandinavia became cooler and wetter, limiting agriculture [14]
- Early Iron Age settlements consisted of small fenced villages [14]
- The Pre-Roman Iron Age covers the 5th-1st centuries BC [1]

**c. 500 BC**: Climate-Driven Migration
- Climate deterioration set the stage for local groups to migrate southward into Germania [14]
- Depletion of cultivated land in the last century BC contributed to increasing migrations in northern Europe [14]
- Increasing conflict between Teutonic tribes and Roman settlements in Gaul [14]

**c. 400-300 BC**: Hjortspring Boat and Warfare
- The Hjortspring boat, the oldest find of a wooden plank ship in Scandinavia, built circa 400-300 BC [16][17]
- Hull and remains excavated in 1921-1922 from Hjortspring Mose on the island of Als in Sønderjylland, southern Denmark [16]
- Vessel is over 19 metres (62 feet) length overall, 13.6 metres (45 feet) long inside, and 2 metres (6.6 feet) wide [16]
- Ten thwarts could have served as seats for crew of at least 20 who propelled the boat with paddles [16]
- Contained weapons and armour, including:
  - 131 shields of Celtic type
  - 33 well-crafted shield bosses
  - 138 iron spearheads
  - 31 bone or antler spearheads
  - 11 single-edged iron swords
  - Remains of several mailcoats [16]
- Weapon offering is the oldest find testifying to seaborne warriors and organized military forces in Denmark [17]
- Approximately 80 shields and twice that number of spears suggest roughly 80 fighters, each armed with a shield and two spears [16]
- Four boatloads of warriors—an army of some 80 fighters [16]
- Interpreted as deliberate votive offering: around 350 BC a hostile army of 80-100 warriors attacked the island of Als [16]
- Victors' sacrifice of the defeated army's equipment, including a dismembered horse, lamb, calf, and two dogs placed with the boat [16]

**c. 120-115 BC**: Cimbri and Teuton Migration
- The Cimbri lived in Jutland, which in classical texts was called the Cimbrian peninsula [18][19]
- Both the Teutones and Cimbri left traces in place names from Jutland (in Thy and Himmerland, respectively) [18]
- Around 120-115 BC, the Cimbri left their original lands around the North Sea due to flooding, known as the Cymbrian flood [18]
- Ancient writers described them variously as Celtic-Gaulish, Germanic, or even Cimmerian people [18]
- Some time before 100 BC, many Cimbri, Teutons, and Ambrones migrated south-east [18]
- After unsuccessful battles with the Boii and other Celtic tribes, they appeared c. 113 BC in Noricum [18]

**113-101 BC**: The Cimbrian War
- The Cimbrian or Cimbric War fought between the Roman Republic and the Germanic and Celtic tribes of the Cimbri, Teutons, Ambrones and Tigurini [19]
- At the Battle of Noreia, they defeated the Romans after being deceived and attacked by Roman consul Gnaeus Papirius Carbo [18]
- 105 BC: Victory at Arausio (Orange) was one of the greatest disasters in the history of Roman warfare [18]
- The Teutones were eventually routed and massacred; their king, Teutobod, was placed in Roman chains [18]
- 101 BC: During attempted invasion of the Italian peninsula, the Cimbri were decisively defeated at the Battle of Vercellae by Gaius Marius; their king, Boiorix, was killed [18][19]

## Roman Iron Age (1 AD - 400 AD)

**1st-2nd Century AD**: Roman Trade and Contact
- By the time of the Iron Age, the Danes had established trade links with the Roman Empire [1]
- Trading goods such as animal furs and amber [1]
- The Amber Road was an ancient trade route for transfer of amber from the North Sea and Baltic Sea to the Mediterranean Sea [20]
- In Roman times, a main route ran south from the Baltic coast through modern Poland, through the land of the Boii to the head of the Adriatic Sea (Aquileia) [20]
- Famous trading expeditions to the Baltic coasts in Denmark and Poland for amber to emperor Nero's palace after the burning of Rome in 64 AD [20]
- Roman goods flowed northward along these routes, exchanged for amber, furs, and slaves [20]
- Other commodities exported to Romans: animal fur and skin, honey, and wax [20]
- Imported from Romans: glass, brass, gold, and non-ferrous metals such as tin and copper [20]

**2nd Century AD**: Navigation Routes
- The Egyptian Geographer Klaudios Ptolemaios' travel descriptions from the 2nd Century describe the naval route from The Rhine around Jutland and through the Danish sea of Isles to the coast of Poland [20]

**c. 200 AD**: Adoption of Runes
- By 200 AD, the Danish people had begun using the Rune language chiseled in stone [1]
- The highest concentration of Elder Futhark inscriptions is in Denmark [21]

**Roman Iron Age**: Denmark as Roman Client State
- Alliances described by historians as Denmark being a "Client State" of Rome [20]
- A supportive territory for the Romans where they traded and recruited Auxilia and soldiers for wars [20]
- More than 11,000 Roman coins found in the region [20]
- Also found: bronze vessels, pots and pans, terra sigillata, glass and much more [20]
- Denmark's location at the crossroads of maritime and overland trade routes made it a crucial hub for exchanges between the Roman Empire and northern European tribes [20]

**400-405 BC**: Tollund Man
- The Tollund Man (died 405-384 BC) is a naturally mummified corpse from the Pre-Roman Iron Age [22]
- Found in 1950, preserved as a bog body near Silkeborg on the Jutland peninsula [22]
- Found with the rope used to strangle him still around his neck [22]
- Cause of death determined to be by hanging [22]
- Believed to be a ritual sacrifice due to special treatment: arranged position, eyes and mouth closed, and special last meal [22]
- More than 500 bog bodies and skeletal remains dating to the Iron Age have been recovered in Denmark [22]

**Roman Iron Age**: Grauballe Man
- Found in Denmark in 1952 [22]
- Most likely killed by having his throat slit [22]
- Corpse deposited in bog where his body was naturally preserved for over two millennia [22]
- Evidence of threshing waste added to last meal, thought to have been killed as human sacrifice [22]
- Iron Age bog bodies show similarities: violent deaths and lack of clothing [22]
- Archaeologists believe they were selected as human sacrifices, possibly to avert disasters like famine [22]

## Germanic Iron Age (400 AD - 700 AD)

**c. 400 AD**: Beginning of Germanic Iron Age
- After Rome's fall, period of post-Roman economic and political reorganization referred to as the Germanic Iron Age, a.d. 400-800 [23][24]
- From around the 5th to 7th century, Northern Europe experienced mass migrations [23]
- This period and its material culture are referred to as the Germanic Iron Age [23]

**c. 400-1000 AD**: Lindholm Høje Burial Site
- Lindholm Høje Iron Age and Viking Age burial site north of Aalborg and Nørresundby on the south slope of Voerbjerg Hill facing Limfjorden [25][26]
- Site in use as burial site between 400 AD and 1000 AD [25]
- Excavated from sand covering in 1952-1958 to reveal 700 graves [25]
- Around 682 cremation graves marked by rocks, making it Scandinavia's largest Viking burial ground [26]
- At each grave, the dead body was burnt on location, and graves marked by stone circles [25]
- Iron Age graves covered with mounds; Viking Age graves were cremation graves with stone-surrounded perimeter [26]
- Majority of burials were cremations, though some inhumations discovered [26]
- Graves arranged chronologically: oldest graves from Iron Age around 400 AD near top of hill [26]
- Northern (higher) part dates to 5th century AD (Nordic Iron Age); southern (lower) part dates to 1000-1050 AD (Viking Age) [26]
- Burial site completely covered by sand in year AD 1000, preserving stone markings [26]

**Early 5th Century (c. 400-450 AD)**: Golden Horns of Gallehus
- Two horns made of sheet gold dated to the early 5th century, beginning of Germanic Iron Age [21][27]
- Discovered in Gallehus, north of Møgeltønder in Southern Jutland
- Horns found in 1639 and 1734, respectively, at locations only 15-20 metres apart [21]
- The shorter horn found in 1734 had six segments with Proto-Norse Elder Futhark inscription at rim [21]
- Inscription reads: "ekhlewagastiR : holtijaR : horna : tawido" [21]
- Translates to: "I Lægæst, son of Holt (or 'from Holt') made the horn" [21]
- Among the earliest Elder Futhark inscriptions recording a full sentence [21]
- Earliest preserving a line of alliterative verse [21]
- Important Proto-Norse inscription, one of the most significant early runic texts [21]
- On 4 May 1802, horns stolen by goldsmith Niels Heidenreich who melted them down to recycle the gold [21]
- Replicas later created based on surviving drawings and documentation [21]

**c. 500 AD**: First Written Records of the Danes
- History of Denmark as a unified kingdom began in the 8th century, but historic documents describe the geographic area and the people—the Danes—as early as 500 AD [1]
- Writings of Jordanes and Procopius mention the Danes [1]

**c. 500 AD**: Genetic Influx from Central Europe
- A 2025 study in Nature found genetic evidence of an influx of central European population after about 500 AD into the region later ruled by the Danes [23][28]
- Later in the Germanic Iron Age—after around 500 and towards the Viking Age—the Danes became recipients of genetic impulses from the south [23]
- Increasingly mixed with people from Austria, France, and Germany who migrated northward [23][28]

**c. 500 AD**: Territorial Distribution of Germanic Tribes
- In the Nordic Iron Age, the Danes were based in present-day Zealand and Scania (and neighbouring parts of present-day Sweden) [23][28]
- Until around the 6th century, Jutland was the homeland of two other Germanic tribes:
  - The Jutes in what is now North Jutland
  - The Angles in South Jutland [23]

**5th-6th Century**: Anglo-Saxon Migration to Britain
- The Jutes originated in modern-day Denmark, believed to have given their name to the Jutland Peninsula [29][30]
- From the Frisian coast they settled southern Britain in the later fifth century during the Migration Period [29]
- Archaeological evidence and research shows early 400s saw mass flooding on coasts of North Germany and Frisia, forcing the Jutes to migrate to Britain [30]
- Denmark witnessed political instability with fragmented land in conflict and new dynasties emerging [30]
- The Angles formed Mercia, East Anglia, and Northumbria [29]
- The Saxons formed Essex, Sussex, and Wessex [29]
- The Jutes settled in the Isle of Wight, Hampshire, and Kent [29]
- By 585, some 200,000 Angles, Saxons and Jutes from Jutland were living in England [29]

**c. 515 AD**: First Danish King Mentioned in European Sources
- Hugleik (Hygelac) suffered a defeat in 515 during a naval expedition to the kingdom of the Franks [31]
- First Danish king mentioned in the European sources [31]

**c. 550 AD**: Political Consolidation
- Around 550 AD, Gothic writings indicate that many small polities in Denmark were being consolidated into bigger political units during the Germanic Iron Age [23]
- According to Jordanes (writing around 550), the Danes came from the East (i.e., from Sweden) and settled on Zealand [28]
- From Zealand, they moved westward to put the Heruli to flight [28]
- The ancient writer Jordanes says that the Dani were of the same stock as the Suetidi (Swedes) and expelled the Heruli, taking their lands [28]

**c. 550-1000 AD**: Lejre - Center of the Scylding Dynasty
- The royal seat and capital of the Danes was located on Zealand near Lejre, constituting what has been dubbed the Lejre Kingdom, ruled by the Skjöldung dynasty [28][32]
- Lejre thought to be the real-world counterpart to Heorot, the royal hall in the Anglo-Saxon poem Beowulf, as home of the Skjölding (Old English: Scylding) dynasty [32]
- According to early legends, ruled by kings of the Skjöldung dynasty, predecessors of medieval Danish kings [32]
- Archaeological excavations since the 1980s produced dramatic confirmation that medieval legends of Lejre have a basis in history [32]
- The Iron Age archaeological settlement complex spans from about 550 to about 1000 AD [32]
- In 2009, remains of Denmark's largest Viking Age house emerged: just over 60 meters from gable to gable (about as long as modern football pitch is wide), with rows of large, thick posts supporting a building with ceiling height over 10 meters [32]
- Site associated with mythical legendary royal family, the Skjoldungs, which according to legend included kings: Skjold, Roar, Helge, Rolf Krake, Ragnar Lodbrog and Harald Hildetand [32]
- Legends known from medieval sources including twelfth-century Gesta Danorum by Saxo Grammaticus and the anonymous Chronicon Lethrense (Chronicle of Lejre) [32]
- Lejre was center for crafts, commerce, and religious observances [32]
- Relative absence of weapon finds suggests site was more important as social and economic center than military base [32]

**6th Century**: Jutes and Angles Part of Danish Kingdom
- Some time around the middle of the First Millennium, both Jutland and Angeln became part of Danish kingdom or kingdoms [28]
- Denmark was long divided into three States or kingdoms with three principal monuments connected with election custom of Kings: at Lund in Skane, Lethra in Zealand, and Viburg in Jutland [28]

**600s-700s AD**: Germanic Iron Age Gold-Rich Period
- The Germanic Iron Age is the gold-richest period in Denmark's history [23]
- The Goths and other migration peoples won gold in battles in southern Europe, earned it as military pay, or shared in large amounts paid by emperors to keep barbarians from attacking the empire [23]
- Some brought the gold back home to their original homeland [23]

**7th Century**: Assimilation of the Jutes
- By the 7th century, Jutish territories in Kent and the Isle of Wight had become integral parts of larger political entities [30]
- The Jutes were eventually assimilated into the broader Danish identity [30]
- By the Viking Age, they had largely ceased to exist as a separate group [30]

**c. 700 AD**: Archaeological Evidence of Unified Denmark
- Archaeological evidence suggests that Denmark was a single entity by the 700s [31]

**c. 710 AD**: Ongendus
- Ongendus (Angantyr) ruled around c. 710 when Saint Willibrord visited the Danes [31]

## Legendary Kings and Early Dynasties (Uncertain Dating)

**Uncertain Dating - Legendary Period**: The Scylding Dynasty
- Dan "Mykillate" founded a new Danish dynasty around AD 275 and married Frovor (Drott), the Danish heiress of the previous Scylding dynasty [31]
- The legendary kings of Denmark were, according to legend, the monarchs of Denmark, the Danes, or specific lands of Denmark (Zealand, Jutland or Scania) who preceded Gorm the Old [31]
- Gorm the Old reigned c. 930s to c. 960s and is the earliest reliably attested Danish ruler [31]
- Kings preceding Gorm may be partly historical (especially those near to Gorm's time), but are either semi-legendary or entirely mythological [31]
- Danish and Nordic legendary stories, chronicles and sagas often have accounts of Danish kings and dynasties stretching further back than the 7th century, but historicity is often doubted [31]

---

**Note**: This timeline synthesizes information from archaeological, historical, and genetic research. Dating for prehistoric periods and legendary figures should be understood as approximate. The transition from legendary to historically documented Danish rulers occurs gradually from the 6th-8th centuries AD.
