# Complete Timeline of the Angles on the Jutland Peninsula

## Prehistoric Period

### 6th-1st centuries BC: Pre-Roman Iron Age
- **Jastorf Culture**: The Angles inhabited regions covered by the Jastorf culture, an Iron Age material culture stretching north into Jutland. This culture formed the southern part of the Pre-Roman Iron Age [1].
- The region that would become known as Angeln (the Angle homeland) was located in the northeastern portion of modern Schleswig-Holstein, on the Jutland Peninsula, formed as a triangle drawn roughly from modern Flensburg on the Flensburger Fjord to the City of Schleswig and then to Maasholm, on the Schlei inlet [2].

### c. 100 BC - 450 AD: Thorsberg Moor Sacrificial Period
- **Thorsberg Moor**: A peat bog near Süderbrarup in Anglia, Schleswig-Holstein, was used as a sacred place of sacrifice by the Angles for approximately four centuries [3].
- The oldest items from Thorsberg, dating to around 100 BC, appear to have been offered to bless the land with fertility. More recent items, dating to around 200 AD, are much more military in nature [3].

## Roman Iron Age (1st-4th centuries AD)

### c. 98 AD: First Written Record
- **Tacitus's Germania**: The earliest surviving written mention of the Angles appears in Chapter 40 of Tacitus's Germania. The Roman historian describes the "Anglii" as one of the more remote Suebic tribes living beyond the Semnones and Langobardi [4][5].
- Tacitus lists seven tribes of Jutland and Holstein who worshipped the goddess Nerthus (Mother Earth): the Reudigni, Aviones, Anglii (Angles), Varini, Eudoses, Suarines, and Nuitones. These tribes were "safe behind ramparts of rivers and woods" [4][6].
- **Nerthus Cult**: Tacitus described ceremonial wagon processions where the goddess's cart, kept on an island in a sacred grove and draped in white cloth, was drawn by heifers. During her procession, no one went to war and all iron objects were locked away [6].

### c. 150 AD: Ptolemy's Geography
- **Claudius Ptolemy's Geography**: Ptolemy's work mentions the Angles (Sueboi Angeilloi or Suevi Angili), though there appears to be confusion in his placement of them. Scholar Gudmund Schütte suggests the Angles were placed correctly northeast of the Langobardi near the Baltic coast, but errors in Ptolemy's sources may have created duplicate entries [7].

### c. 200 AD: Settlement Development
- **Grøntoft Settlement**: A rural settlement in western Jutland dating from about 200 AD reveals a "wandering village" that remained in the same resource territory for approximately three hundred years [8].
- **Iron-Working**: Iron-working techniques were generally mastered around 200-100 BC, and by 200 AD, a productive smithing industry had evolved in the larger settlements. Bog ore was abundant in southwestern Jutland [8].

### 200-400 AD: Peak of Ritual Activity
- **Nydam Bog Offerings**: The Nydam bog became a major sacred site where weapons and ships of vanquished armies were offered to indigenous gods. Many items were deliberately destroyed in ritual sacrificial acts during this period [9].
- Archaeological evidence from this period provides detailed insights into Angle civilization, including weapons, ornaments, articles of clothing, and agricultural implements [9][10].

### c. 310-320 AD: Nydam Boat
- **Construction of Nydam Boat**: A 23-meter (75 feet) long oak vessel was constructed and later deposited in the Nydam moor as a votive offering. The boat has been dendrochronologically dated to this period. It was clinker-built, weighed over three tonnes, had 14 pairs of oars, and likely had a crew of around 45 men [9].

## Migration Period

### 3rd Century: Beginning of Great Migration
- **Migration Period Begins**: The Great Migration of Peoples began in the 3rd century, though the most intense migration would occur in the middle of the 5th century [11].

### 4th-5th Centuries: Archaeological Evidence of Angles
- **Cremation Cemeteries**: The Angles practiced cremation as their predominant burial method during the 4th and 5th centuries. Large cremation cemeteries flourished at sites such as:
  - Borgstedt (between Rendsburg and Eckernförde)
  - Hammoor (Germany)
  - Sørup (Denmark)
  - Bordesholm and Süderbrarup in Schleswig-Holstein [12][13]
- Design motifs on ceramics from Continental Anglian cremation cemeteries show strong similarities to vessels later found in England, indicating cultural continuity during migration [12].

### Pre-Migration: Legendary Kings
- **Kings of Angeln**: According to legendary traditions preserved in the heroic poems Widsith and Beowulf, and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, the Angles in their continental homeland were ruled by a succession of kings, including:
  - **Wermund**: A king whose fortress at Schleswig was captured by the Myrgings (a branch of Saxons) [14]
  - **Offa of Angel**: Son of Wermund, who retook the fortress and secured the Angles' southern border with the Saxons along the River Eider. His combat took place at Rendsburg on an island in the Eider River at Fifeldore (Monster-Gate), where he killed both his opponents [14].
  - These legendary rulers connected the later Mercian royal family to their Anglian homeland [14].

## Mass Migration to Britain

### c. 440-450 AD: First Substantial Migrations
- **Initial Migration Wave**: The first substantial migrations of the Angles, along with Saxons and Jutes, to Britain occurred around 450-500 CE [15].
- Bede gave a precise date of 449 AD for the first arrival of the Anglo-Saxons, though this traditional date has been debated by scholars [15].

### 5th-6th Centuries: Mass Exodus and Depopulation
- **Depopulation of Angeln**: Settlement density in Angeln dramatically decreased in the 5th and 6th centuries. Many villages fell into disrepair as the Angles departed for Britain [16].
- **Archaeological Evidence**: By the 6th century, the large Continental cremation cemeteries were no longer in use, and settlement activity disappeared between the 5th and 8th centuries [12].
- **Bede's Account (c. 731 AD)**: The Venerable Bede, in his Ecclesiastical History of the English People, stated that the Angles came "from the country which is called Angulus, and which is said, from that time, to have remained desert to this day, between the provinces of the Jutes and the Saxons" [17][18].

### c. 450-600 AD: Active Migration and Settlement Period
- **Continued Migration**: Active migration and settlement in Britain continued approximately from 450-600 CE [15].
- **Settlement Patterns**: According to Bede, the Angles settled in East Anglia, while the Saxons settled in southern England, and the Jutes in Kent and the Isle of Wight [15].
- **Genetic Evidence**: DNA analysis of 494 people who died in England between 400 and 900 CE shows they derived more than three-quarters of their ancestry from Northern Europe, suggesting substantial migration rather than just a small warrior elite [15].

### Post-Migration Period

### 8th Century: Danish Occupation
- **Danish Settlement**: Following the departure of the Angles, the region of Angeln was eventually occupied by Danes who had migrated south from Scandinavia [16].
- By the mid-5th century, the Danes had established settlements from Grenen in the north to just north of the Eider River, integrating the remaining Jutes and Angles who had not left for Britain [19].

### c. 731 AD: Bede's Historical Account
- **Ecclesiastical History Completed**: The Venerable Bede completed his Ecclesiastical History of the English People, preserving the memory of the Angles' continental homeland and confirming that Angeln remained largely depopulated centuries after the migration [17][18].

## Legacy

The Angles left an indelible mark on history despite their departure from the Jutland Peninsula:

- **Etymology**: Their name, derived from the Angeln peninsula, became the root of the name "England" ("Engla land" - "Land of the Angles") and "English" [20].
- **Archaeological Heritage**: Sites like Thorsberg moor and Nydam continue to provide crucial evidence about Germanic Iron Age culture, religion, and technology [3][9].
- **Cultural Continuity**: Religious practices such as the Nerthus cult may have survived among the Angles into Christian times in England, as evidenced by the partially-Christianized pagan Anglo-Saxon Æcerbot ritual [6].
- **Genetic Legacy**: Modern genetic analysis shows that the ancestors of Anglo-Saxon samples are closest to modern Danish and Dutch populations, confirming the continental Germanic origins of the English people [8].
