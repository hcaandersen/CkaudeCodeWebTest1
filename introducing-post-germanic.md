## Introducing the Term: *Post-Germanic*

By **Hans Andersen**

In everyday conversation we speak of "Western common sense" as if it were universal: as if Westerners simply see the world as it *is*, and everyone everywhere else just doesn't. But what if what we call "common sense" is not universal at all — what if it is deeply **cultural**, and shaped by a particular historical trajectory? I propose the term **post-Germanic** to capture one of the key strands shaping this trajectory: the social, political and moral logic inherited from the Germanic migrations and aristocratic warrior cultures of early medieval Europe, which then became embedded in Western institutions and mind-sets.

By *post-Germanic* I mean: the aristocratic, warrior-landholding, hierarchical form of society that emerged from the Germanic tribal conquests and migrations (5th-8th c) and persisted (in adapted form) into the medieval, early-modern and modern West. These aristocracies shaped the underlying assumptions about property, loyalty, hierarchy, conquest, order and the self that many Westerners now regard as "just common sense."

In what follows I will (1) show how Germanic warrior culture gave rise to aristocratic forms that undergird Western institutions, (2) argue how these forms helped mould Western common sense, and (3) explain why this matters when Westerners engage with non-Western societies (for example, China), since their default assumptions may not translate.

---

## 1. The Germanic → Aristocracy Trajectory

### 1.1 Warrior bonding and the *comitatus*

In the Germanic tribal world, one of the core social bonds was the war-band or retinue (Latin *comitatus*) — the loyalty of armed retainers to their leader, pledged in battle, rewarded by gifts, bound by honour. ([Tacitus, *Germania*][1]) For example, Tacitus described in his *Germania* how Germanic chiefs attracted companions by generosity, and how battle-glory mattered. ([Tacitus, *Germania*][1])

That war-band logic furnished later feudal institutions: the bond of lord and vassal, the grant of land for service, the expectation of loyalty in return for protection. ([historum.com][2])

### 1.2 Germanic kingship, patrimony and aristocracy

Germanic kingship as described in early medieval scholarship combined sacral/ritual elements, war-leadership, patrimonial landholding and a personal link with the warriors. ([Wallace-Hadrill][3]) Over time, the Germanic elites (Anglo-Saxons, Franks, Goths, Lombards) took over Roman-Imperial territories, merged with Roman law and Christian religion, and produced hybrid aristocracies: warrior-nobles who claimed land, status and hereditary rights.

### 1.3 Feudalism and the landed warrior aristocrat

By the 9th–15th centuries, Western Europe operated under a social and military system known as *feudalism* (lords, vassals, fiefs, land for service) which, while influenced by Roman patrimonial models, bears strong traces of the earlier Germanic warrior culture. ([Bloch][4]) For instance: decentralised power, land-allotments to mounted warriors, personal oaths of loyalty. These features argue for a continuity (not simply importation) from the Germanic past into the medieval aristocratic order.

## 2. What "Post-Germanic" Means for Western Common Sense

### 2.1 The cultural logic

If one descends from a warrior aristocracy that holds land, loyalty, hierarchy, inheritance and warfare as givens, then one tends to adopt certain assumptions as *natural*. Examples:

* Property is personal and inviolable.
* Service and loyalty are owed to a superior, but exchangeable (you serve, you get protection / reward).
* Order is maintained through hierarchy, status and obedience.
* Conquest and expansion are legitimate forms of asserting status.
* The individual (or family/lineage) is an active agent, not simply part of a collective.

These assumptions have become "common sense" in many Western-derived societies — so much so that they are often *unexamined*.

### 2.2 Why the Roman lineage alone doesn't suffice

The common public narrative of the West often emphasises the Roman legacy: law, citizenship, republican virtue, rational order. Of course, these **are** important. But focusing solely on Rome misses the **warrior-aristocratic substrate** that the Germanic migrations inserted into Western social structure (especially in Anglo-Saxon England, German lands, France). The Germanic element gave a different set of impulses: patrimony, personal loyalty, land-for-service, dynamic frontiers and war-leadership rather than bureaucratic governance.

### 2.3 The term "post-Germanic" as analytic tool

By describing Western culture as *post-Germanic*, I mean that the warrior aristocratic logic did not end with the migrations — it was transformed, institutionalised, and persists (in modified form) in Western social, political and economic habits. It is "post" in the sense of evolved from, succeeded by, rather than completely replaced. Thus the classes, norms, and common-sense assumptions we have today are (in part) the legacy of those early Germanic aristocracies. The term helps emphasise the continuity of the *style of thinking* rather than just the ethnic or racial dimension.

---

## 3. Why This Matters in a Global Context

When Western people engage with non-Western societies (such as China, India, Africa), they often unconsciously apply their "common sense" assumptions — e.g., notions of individual property rights, linear progress, rational self-interest, hierarchical service-contracts — believing them to be universal. But non-Western societies may operate under different logics: collective responsibility, institutional patrimony, network-based loyalty, non-linear time horizons, different conceptions of hierarchy and service.

Recognising that Western common sense is in part *post-Germanic* helps one understand why misunderstandings occur: Westerners may assume that their cultural logic is self-evident, when in fact it springs from a particular historical formation. When Westerners say "we believe in individual liberty, in contracts, in change" and assume everyone else must see that too, they may overlook that these are not universal axioms — they were shaped by a specific cultural moment (the Germanic + medieval aristocratic synthesis) and have been globalised.

---

## 4. Key Caveats & Further Research

* **Not inevitable**: The Germanic aristocracies did not determine everything. Other influences (Roman law & governance, Christianity, Byzantine models, Islamic civilisation, etc.) play major roles.
* **Complexity**: The term "Germanic" covers many tribes and cultures; the transition from Germanic tribal culture to medieval aristocracy is neither simple nor uniform. Some scholars caution about generalising. ([Wallace-Hadrill][3])
* **Empirical backing**: If you adopt the term you should be prepared to engage with nuance: when and how the Germanic patterns persisted, how they merged with Roman or Christian ideas, how they diverged in different regions (Britain, France, Germany, Iberia).
* **Alternative logics**: Non-Western societies have their own rich heritages (Confucian, Buddhist, Islamic, tribal, etc). The post-Germanic lens is a tool for self-understanding, not a universal framework.

---

## Conclusion

The term **post-Germanic** offers a way to think about Western common sense as a historically specific product of warrior-aristocratic, land-holding, hierarchical societies born of the Germanic migrations and transformed through medieval Europe. By making this ancestry explicit we gain clearer self-understanding — and we stand better positioned to recognise that when engaging globally, our "common sense" may not translate.
